# Why Let's Practice looks the way it does

[Читать по-русски](DESIGN.ru.md)

Let's Practice is made for every child, including children with ADHD, autism, dyslexia or dyscalculia. Every design
decision below has a reason. Where there is research we link it; where there is only good practice we say so.
**These worksheets are not a treatment or a therapy.** They try not to get in the way of learning.

## 1. Black and white, no decorations

**Why.** Decorations that do not carry information pull attention away from the task.

- Kindergarten children taught in a classroom with heavily decorated walls were more distracted, spent more time off
  task and learned less than when the decorations were removed ([Fisher, Godwin & Seltman, 2014][fisher]).
- In an eye-tracking study, busy classroom displays distracted all children, and children with autism more; time spent
  looking at the displays was the strongest predictor of poorer learning ([Hanley et al., 2017][hanley]).
- Adults and children learn better when extraneous material is left out — the *coherence principle*; Mayer reports it
  supported in nearly all experimental tests, with a large median effect ([Mayer, multimedia learning principles][mayer]).
  Interesting but irrelevant details ("seductive details") reduce learning in a small-to-moderate way
  (Rey, 2012, meta-analysis; [overview][rey]).
- Colour must never be the only way to show something: about 1 in 12 boys has a colour vision deficiency
  ([NHS / nidirect][cvd]). This is also a rule of web accessibility ([WCAG 1.4.1, Use of colour][wcag]).
- Black and white prints on any printer, costs least, and leaves the colouring to the child.

**Where we do highlight.** Cues that show *what matters* help learning (Mayer's *signalling principle*), so we keep a
few, without colour: the worked example sits on **grey**, the start of a number to trace is a **bigger dot**, the task
number is a **black circle**.

## 2. The first item is already done (grey = "do it like this")

Studying a worked example before solving similar problems helps beginners learn — the *worked-example effect* of
cognitive load theory ([Atkinson et al., 2000][atkinson]; [overview][worked]). The effect becomes smaller for children who
already know the topic (the *expertise reversal effect*), so there is only **one** example per task, the rest is practice.

## 3. One picture = one action

Children who cannot read yet follow a small, fixed set of action icons (count, circle, match, colour, trace, write, tick,
cross out, draw). *Visual supports* are one of the evidence-based practices for autistic children and young people
in the 2020 NCAEP review (over 100 studies) ([NCAEP 2020][ncaep]; [AFIRM visual supports][afirm]). The same icon always means
the same action, and every task has the same layout — predictability is good practice for autistic children and
helpful for everyone, although we know of no study of *worksheet* layout specifically.

## 4. Little on a page, one idea at a time

Few items per task, a lot of white space, short instructions, one action per task. Breaking work into small parts with
a clear start and end is common advice for children with ADHD ([CHADD][chadd]).
**Honest note:** a systematic review found that most classroom accommodations for ADHD lack ADHD-specific evidence
([Lovett & Nelson, 2021][lovett]). We treat these choices as low-cost good design, not as an intervention.

## 5. "I can do it!" box

Every cheat-sheet card ends with a box the child ticks. Self-regulation approaches such as self-monitoring improve
on-task behaviour of children with ADHD; effects on academic results are smaller
([meta-analysis of classroom interventions for ADHD, 2016][classroom]; a review of 41 self-monitoring studies by Bruhn,
McDaniel & Kreigh, 2015, is summarised in [this 2019 study][selfmon]).

## 6. Fonts and text

Nunito, a rounded sans-serif font, large sizes (task text 19–21 px, numbers 24–34 px), no italics, left-aligned short
lines. This follows dyslexia-friendly style guidance: plain sans-serif fonts, at least 12–14 pt, no patterned
backgrounds ([British Dyslexia Association style guide, as summarised by Dyslexia Scotland][bda]).
**One difference:** the guidance suggests a light but *not pure white* background. On paper you can simply print on
cream or pastel paper; an off-white screen option is on our list.

## 7. Maths pictures

Ten frames, dice patterns (to recognise up to 5 without counting — a Reception goal), part–whole models and number lines
are the representations used in the government's maths guidance for Year 1 ([DfE, 2020][dfe]) and recommended in the
early maths guidance of the Education Endowment Foundation ([EEF][eef]). For dyscalculia specifically we found no strong
evidence about worksheets; these representations are the standard first step in UK schools.

## 8. What we do not claim

- Most studies above are about all children or small groups; few are about printed worksheets.
- Children are different. If something does not work for *your* child, please tell us —
  [suggest a change](https://github.com/ISergey256/lets-practice-worksheets/issues/new/choose).
